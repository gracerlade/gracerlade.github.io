How Much Do College Volleyball Teams Rely on Their Top Attackers, and Does It Impact Their Success?

volleyball
ncaa
statistics
Author

Grace Lade

Published

November 13, 2026

There is always a debate of what makes a volleyball team good. Is it aces, kills, or no errors? What about having a singular player who dominates for the whole team? A player that is consistent and can produce kills is something that every college volleyball team can benefit from. But there is a difference between a team that has a leading attacker and having an offense that strictly relies on one. When a single player is responsible for the bulk of the team’s kills, it raises questions about how much of the team’s offense is dependent on one player versus the rest of the roster.

Looking at the 2025 women’s college volleyball season, there are many examples on how differently these teams rely on their players, or lets just say one player. When you look at the data from the Big Ten, SEC, ACC, Big 12, and the Big East, the kill amount and kill percentage vary. Some teams are well-rounded with an even distribution of kills across the board, where on the other hand some heavily rely on one athlete who are a big part of their offense. Miami (FL), is an extreme example of a team who leans on one player. Flormarie Heredia Colon is responsible for 779 kills during the 2025 season, which accounts for over 46.5% of Miami’s total kills. What does this mean? Nearly one of every two kills was done by the same player, Colon. On the other hand, Penn State’s Kennedy Martin attributed 38.7% of her team’s kills, and LSU’s Jurnee Robinson accounted for 38.1%. These players were a significant part of their team’s success, but neither of them were close to Miami’s Flormarie Heredia Colon’s percentage.

Finishing up with Notre Dame, Colorado and Pittsburg who also appeared on the top of rankings, with their leading attackers who contributed more than a third of their team’s kills.

When looking at the percentage of total kills a player has for a team, it is easy to make assumptions about what kind of player they are and what team they are on. However, it doesn’t always give the right answer about the rest of the team. When looking at the difference of kills between a team’s top attacker versus their second best, there is a wide variety of what that gap looks like. If you look at Pittsburgh, they are a good example of a team who has a much larger gap between their top attackers. Olivia Babcock records over 645 kills, whereas their next best, only has a bit over 300 kills. Nebraska, on the other hand, has a much smaller difference. Their highest attacker only records around 380 kills, and its lowest around 290.

Some teams do have larger gaps, and while this may seem concerning, it doesn’t necessarily mean there should be cause for concern, or that a team’s offense isn’t as good. There are many factors that go into a player’s stats, some of which could include playing time, strength of the roster for a certain position, etc. But, the difference does show what it looks like when a team relies more on one person versus distributing more.

Code 
players <- read_csv("wvb_playerseason_div1_2025.csv")
teams <- read_csv("wvb_teamseason_div1_2025.csv")

powerfour <- c("ACC", "Big 12", "Big East", "Big Ten", "SEC")

playerkills <- players |> 
  filter(Conference %in% c("ACC", "Big Ten", "Big 12", "SEC", "Big East")) |>
  filter(Team %in% c("Miami(FL)", "Penn St.", "LSU", "Notre Dame", "Pittsburgh", "Nebraska", "Texas", "Texas A&M", "Wisconsin", "Creighton","Kentucky", "Purdue", "SMU", "Stanford", "Arizona St")) |> 
  group_by(Team, Conference, Player) |>
  summarise(Kills= sum(Kills)) |> 
  group_by(Team, Conference) |> 
  top_n(2, wt=Kills)

top <- playerkills |> 
  group_by(Team) |> 
  filter(Kills == max(Kills)) |> 
  select(Team, Conference, Kills) |> 
  rename(TopKills = Kills)

bottom <- playerkills |> 
  group_by(Team) |> 
  filter(Kills == min(Kills)) |> 
  select(Team, Conference, Kills) |> 
  rename(MinKills = Kills)

topandbottom <- top |> inner_join(bottom)

ggplot() + 
  geom_segment(
    data=topandbottom, 
    aes(
      y=Team, yend=Team,
      x=MinKills, xend=TopKills),
    color="grey",
    linewidth = 1.5,
  ) + 
  geom_point(
    data=topandbottom,
    aes(
      y=Team,
      x=MinKills,
      color="Player with the Least Kills"
    ),
    size=2
  ) + 
  geom_point(
    data=topandbottom,
    aes(
      y=Team,
      x=TopKills,
    color="Player with the Most Kills"
    ),
    size=2
  )  + 
  scale_color_manual(name="Volleyball Kill Range", values=c("red", "green")) + 
  labs(
    x="Total Kills", 
    y="Team", 
    title="The Gap Between NCAA Volleyball Teams’ \nHighest and Lowest Kill Totals", 
    subtitle="Comparing the highest and lowest individual kill totals across 15 dominant volleyball teams.", 
    caption="Source: NCAA Volleyball | By Grace Lade"
    ) +
  theme_minimal() + 
  theme(
    plot.title = element_text(size = 20, face = "bold"),
    axis.title = element_text(size = 8), 
    plot.subtitle = element_text(size=10), 
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    plot.title.position = "plot"
    )

Does relying on one player affect the total number of kills a team has? Looking at Purdue, they produced the most kills in the 2025 season, with more than 1,800 kills. Kentucky followed close behind, with SMU, Texas A&M and Pittsburgh ranking towards the top. Having one player who is putting up high numbers is something a good team can use to their advantage, but it’s worthy to note that this can put a lot on one person to perform. When you look at a team who has a fairly even distribution of power, it’s easy to assume that one player isn’t taking on as much responsibility.

So what makes a collegiate volleyball team good? Is it having one player who can consistently dominate on the offense, or having an entire roster that can perform when needed? Both answers, yes and no, can be defended with evidence. However, these charts point at the obvious answer, teams who are successful rely on much more than just kills.
